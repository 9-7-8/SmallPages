Pomodoro Timer

Work for a set stretch, break for a shorter one, and take a longer break every few rounds — with all of the lengths yours to set. The page goes the colour of whichever phase it is in, so a glance from across the room says whether you are working or not, without reading a single digit.

The background is the phase. Deep red while you are working, green on a short break, blue on a long one — and each one brightens a little as its phase runs down, so the room says roughly how far through you are before you have looked at the clock. Hue carries the signal and the lightness travels only a few points, so the page stays a dark ground throughout rather than lighting the room up. Until the first start it is black like every other page here, and a reset puts it back.

It counts from the clock, not from its own ticks. Every phase is a timestamp to land on rather than a thousand additions, and the next phase is measured from the moment the last one was due to end rather than from when the page noticed — so nothing drifts and the schedule stays the schedule. Browsers throttle a tab you are not looking at, and a locked phone stops it altogether; come back and the page works out which phase you ought to be in, says how many it caught up on, and carries on from the right place.

The chime is made here, not played from a file. Struck tones from the browser's own audio engine — rising into a break, falling back into work, and a longer fall when the set is done. Browsers will not let a page make sound until you have asked it to, which is why the first start is also what builds it, and a chime that falls due while the tab is asleep arrives late or not at all. The colour and the clock are the signals to trust.

Skip and reset mean different things. Skip moves to the next phase without counting the one you left, so a focus run you abandon earns no pip and brings the long break no closer. Reset stops everything, empties the set and clears the tally. The tally counts real running time only — a paused timer is not focus — and it lives no longer than the tab does.

Twenty-five minutes is a convention, not a finding. It is the length Francesco Cirillo happened to set a kitchen timer to in the late eighties, and the method is named after the tomato that timer was shaped like. Nothing measured says twenty-five is the right number for you, which is why every length here is a box you can type in: the part worth keeping is the shape of it — one thing at a time, for a decided stretch, then stop. The settings are kept in this browser so the page opens where you left it; nothing else is kept, and nothing leaves the page.
