Image Compressor

Shrink a picture before you send it, and see exactly what you gave up for the saving — drag the seam and the original is on one side, the compressed on the other, at the same size on the same screen. The encoding is the browser's own, so nothing is uploaded and nothing is queued behind anybody else's job.

What it actually does. The picture is drawn onto a canvas at whatever size you asked for and handed back to the browser's own encoder. That is the same encoder behind every photo your browser has ever saved, and on photographs it is within a few per cent of what a dedicated tool will get you. It is not as good as a modern purpose-built encoder on flat graphics and screenshots, where a real PNG optimiser still wins.

PNG will often come out bigger. Canvas writes a plain PNG with no palette reduction and no filter search, so a PNG that came from a tool which did both will usually grow here. PNG is in the list for the case where you need lossless output or an alpha channel; if the file is a photograph and you want it smaller, WebP at 75 or so beats JPEG at the same apparent quality by a comfortable margin.

Everything but the pixels is dropped. Re-encoding loses the EXIF block, which takes the camera model, the timestamp and the GPS coordinates with it. That is usually what you want before sending a photo on, but it also means the rotation flag is gone — the browser applies it while drawing, so the picture comes out the right way up and stays that way.

Once is free, twice is not. A JPEG re-saved as a JPEG is being compressed on top of compression, and the artefacts compound even at high quality. Go back to the original each time rather than compressing yesterday's output again.
