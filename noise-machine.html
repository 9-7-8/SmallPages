Noise Machine

Four noises to work, sleep or think through, built from scratch in the page by the browser's own audio engine, with a drum that can beat under them and two ways of hearing a beat that are not noise at all. Nothing is downloaded, because there is no recording — it is all generated as it plays. Start from one of the settings along the top, or move everything yourself; whatever you end up with is in the address bar, so it can be bookmarked or sent to somebody.

Drum

Binaural beats

Headphones, or this does nothing. The beat is not in the sound — it is two steady tones, one per ear, and the throb you hear is your own hearing reconciling them. Played through speakers they meet in the air instead and you get an ordinary tremolo, which is a different thing that happens to sound similar.

What the colours mean. White noise has the same energy at every frequency, which to an ear that hears high frequencies with far more resolution sounds like a hiss, weighted toward the top. Pink drops 3 dB per octave as it climbs and lands about where nature does — rain, wind, a river — which is why it is the one most people can leave on. Brown drops 6 dB per octave and is nearly all bottom end: a distant waterfall, or the inside of an aeroplane.

Isochronic tone

The fan is noise, shaped. Brown under a low-pass that breathes, with a quiet tone at the blade rate underneath — a real fan has one, and leaving it out is what makes filtered noise sound like filtered noise. It is not a recording, so there is nothing to recognise on the second pass.

Two ways to hear a beat. The binaural pair puts a steady tone in each ear and lets your hearing find the difference between them, which is why it needs headphones and does nothing through a speaker. The isochronic tone is one tone switched on and off instead, so the pulse is really in the air and a speaker carries it perfectly well; it is also the blunter of the two, and more tiring to sit under. Its edges are ramped rather than square, because a tone cut dead is a click and a click ten times a second is a buzz, and the ramps are a proportion of the on-window so they can never outgrow the pulse they belong to. The duty is how much of each cycle the tone is present for. Depth is how far the level falls between pulses, and it carries a naming rule rather than just a number: at full depth the tone reaches silence, which is what the word isochronic means, and anything short of that never actually stops, so the page calls it a tremolo instead. At nothing it is the bare carrier, which is the honest way to hear what the pulsing is adding. Everything said in the note above about what these are supposed to do to a listener applies here in the same measure, which is to say not much.

Why the beat needs two ears. Nothing in the signal pulses. There are two steady sine tones, say 195 Hz and 205 Hz, and the ten-hertz throb appears only where they are compared, which is well inside the head. That is also why the two channels are kept apart with a merger rather than a pair of panners: panning is a mix, so at anything short of hard over a little of each tone reaches the wrong ear and the pair beat together acoustically on the way in. Separate inputs on a merger are separate channels, and nothing in the graph afterwards folds them back together. Through speakers the air does the mixing instead and you get an ordinary tremolo — a real effect, an audibly similar one, and not the same thing.

On what these are supposed to do. The bands are named after rhythms seen in an EEG, and the usual claim is that a beat at one of those rates pulls the brain toward it. The first half of that is solid: a periodic sound really does produce a measurable, time-locked response. The second half does not follow from the first, and the figures are worth knowing. A systematic review of binaural beats found five studies supporting entrainment, eight not, and one mixed. A 2024 study put an isochronic-derived stimulus against binaural beats and white noise over four minutes in 28 people: the EEG differed, and what the participants reported about their own relaxation did not. For sleep, a review of 34 studies covering 1,103 people found no clear evidence for white or pink noise — while ordinary music before bed has a Cochrane review behind it, moderate-certainty, 10 studies and 708 people. The best-supported noise finding is narrower than it sounds: a meta-analysis of 13 studies and 335 young people found a small gain on attention tasks for those with ADHD symptoms, and the pooled result for everyone else points the other way, slightly worse. Even 40 Hz, the one rate with real research behind it, is mostly research into flickering light with clicking sound in Alzheimer's work, not an audio track. Detecting a rhythmic response is a much narrower claim than putting a brain into a chosen mode, and nothing here is the latter.

It needs the first click. Browsers will not let a page make sound until you have asked it to, which is also why the page cannot start itself when you open it. Leaving the tab in the background is fine; locking the phone usually is not, since the browser suspends the audio engine with everything else.

On volume. Noise that is loud enough to mask a room is loud enough to matter over eight hours. If you are running this overnight, set it to the quietest level that still does the job rather than the level that sounds right in the first minute.
