Ruler

A ruler at its real size, and a protractor to go with it: millimetres or sixteenths of an inch along a straight edge, degrees around a dial. Nothing can tell a browser how big your screen actually is, so the page works out what it can from the screen itself and then lets you settle it against something real — a bank card, a coin, a sheet of A4 — and remembers what you said.

What the browser will say for itself

Why it has to ask. A CSS pixel is defined as a ninety-sixth of an inch, and on a great many screens it is nothing of the sort: a phone runs about 160 of them to the inch, a 4K monitor at no scaling about 163, a Retina laptop about 113. A browser will tell you how many device pixels go into one of its pixels and how many of its pixels go across the screen; what it will never tell you is how many inches that screen is, because it does not know. So everything above is arithmetic on top of one number you have to supply once — and the figure under this scale implies is there to catch a wrong one: if the page reckons your screen is nineteen inches across the diagonal and it is plainly a laptop, the scale is wrong rather than the laptop.

Zoom is already accounted for. What gets kept is the screen's own density in device pixels to the inch, which does not change when you zoom; the pixels to the inch on the page is that divided by the device pixel ratio, and zooming changes the ratio. So a ruler calibrated at one zoom level stays true at another. Dragging the window to a second monitor of a different density is the case this cannot catch, because the browser reports the move the same way it reports a zoom.

The ticks are snapped to whole device pixels so that every line on the scale is one hard pixel rather than two grey ones. That moves each tick by up to half a device pixel — a twentieth of a millimetre on a dense screen, a tenth on a sparse one — and the error does not accumulate along the rule, because every tick is placed from nought rather than from the one before it.

Against the glass, not against a window. The rule runs the whole width of the page rather than sitting in the column the rest of this is written in, so its nought is as near the left edge of the screen as the browser will put it. Fullscreen takes the last of it; Escape comes back out, and the reading rides on the rule beside the mark so that it is there either way. A rule is only ever as long as the glass, which is why the line under it says how long this one came out.

It turns. Drag the knob at the far end to swing the rule about its nought, or type the angle; the square at the nought end slides the whole thing about, so a measurement can start wherever the thing being measured does. Up and down turn it a degree at a time and Shift makes that fifteen, dragging included. Upright is ninety degrees and nothing else: the rule is drawn in its own turned frame, so there is no separate vertical ruler here to disagree with the flat one. Turned into a corner it reaches furthest — the longest thing this page can measure is the diagonal of the screen.

The radial one is a protractor and a radial rule at once: the dial is marked every degree with the figures every ten, the rings are a centimetre or an inch apart, and the scale along nought is the straight rule's own, to the same smallest division. Drag anywhere in it to swing the arm, and the arm's angle and its length are both read out underneath. Nought at the right counts anticlockwise, the way a protractor is printed; nought at the top counts clockwise, the way a bearing is quoted.
