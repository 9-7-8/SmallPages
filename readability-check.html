Pulling hardest

What these formulas actually measure. Every one of them is arithmetic on two things: how long your sentences are and how long your words are. None of them reads. A page of short sentences made of short words scores beautifully whether it is clear or gibberish, and a well-built long sentence scores badly for being long. Use the number to find the places worth looking at — which is what the marks above are for — and then judge those by reading them.

Five, because any one of them is noisy. The grade at the top is the middle of the five, which is steadier than any single formula on a short text. Flesch-Kincaid and the Automated Readability Index weigh sentence length heavily; Gunning Fog and SMOG care about words of three syllables or more; Coleman-Liau counts letters instead of syllables, so it is the one that does not depend on the guesswork below. Flesch Reading Ease is the odd one out — it runs 0 to 100 upwards, so it is reported but not averaged in.

SMOG wants thirty sentences. It was built on samples of thirty and it is unreliable under about that; the page says so when your text is shorter, and leaves it out of the middle.

Syllables are counted by rule, and rules are wrong sometimes. English spelling does not say how many syllables a word has — create is two and greatest is two, spelled almost the same way. The counter here handles the common patterns and carries a list of the words it would otherwise get wrong, and it agrees with a hand count on the ordinary vocabulary of a handout. It will still miss on names, on loanwords and on anything invented. A word or two either way moves the grade by about a tenth.

Sentences are found by rule too. A full stop ends a sentence unless it is on Mr., e.g., an initial or a decimal point; a line break ends one as well, so a heading or a bullet counts as its own sentence rather than running into the next. Lists therefore score easier than prose, which is true of how they read but worth knowing before you compare a worksheet against an essay.
