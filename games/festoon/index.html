Festoon

A rail of lights on long flexes. Sweep your hand through them and they gather, swing and knock together — and when two bulbs knock, the one going faster paints its colour onto the other. Get enough of them matching without smashing the glass.

    How it goes

    Move the pointer over the room, or hold the arrow keys. Everything within the ring is drawn toward your hand, so a slow pass gathers the flexes and a quick one throws them. Nothing happens until you start; a festoon left alone hangs still for ever.

    • Colour travels by contact, and only on the knock. Two bulbs that meet settle which of them changes, and the colour rule above the room says how. Once they are touching they are done arguing: they have to come apart and meet again. A bulb that has just changed also sits out a quarter of a second, so a pair rattling against each other does not flicker between the two.
    • Speed settles it. Of two bulbs that knock, the one going faster paints the other — and the difference does not have to be much, so a bunch gathered in your hand argues itself down to whatever colour it is mostly made of. Only two bulbs going at very nearly the same speed leave each other alone. A bulb moving enough to matter is drawn with a ring round it, and the readout counts them.
    • Glass breaks. Meet hard enough and both bulbs crack, and a knock that hard passes no colour either — it is too busy breaking. A bulb with two cracks goes dark: it still hangs there and still gets in the way, but it is out of the game and can neither take a colour nor give one. Let more go dark than the festoon allows and that is the end of it. Whipping the lights about is not a short cut and not a waste of time either: it sometimes reaches the count before the glass gives and about as often loses the festoon, which is the only gamble on offer here.
    • You win when enough of them match. Each festoon says how many — nine of the ten on Porch, sixteen of the twenty-six on Cathedral — and the number is a count rather than a share, so breaking bulbs never brings it closer. Every bulb on one colour is not the goal and is not meant to be: a festoon is a row, each bulb only ever meets the one beside it, and the last two of the wrong colour sit at a boundary that wanders. Time is the score.

    The colour rules

    • Catch — the faster bulb paints the slower. The plain one: pick the colour you want up and carry it into the lights standing still.
    • Settle — the slower bulb paints the faster. Everything you learn about aiming under Catch is backwards here: you are not delivering a colour, you are posting bulbs into one that is already sitting still.
    • Blend — the colours in play sit on a wheel, and two bulbs that meet each take one step round it toward the other, so a red and a green meet at the amber between them, whoever was going faster. Only when they are already neighbours does speed decide it. With two colours in play every pair is already neighbours, so Blend and Catch are the same game on Porch.
    • Fade — Catch, but a borrowed colour only lasts seven seconds and then the bulb goes back to the colour it started as. The count has to be met at one moment, so this is the hurried one.

    The palette only changes how it looks: the shape on each bulb carries the same information as its colour, and never moves between palettes — the globe is always the first colour, the drop always the second. Beacon is the one to pick if the hues are hard to tell apart.

    Space starts and pauses, R restarts the same festoon, N hangs a new one. The arrow keys or WASD move your hand, which is slower than a pointer but steadier, and steady is worth a lot under Settle.

    What it is not

    Not a lighting rig and not a cloth simulation. A flex is a chain of beads held at a fixed distance from each other by a few passes of a solver, which is cheap and stable and quite wrong: a real cable resists bending, this one does not, so a flex folds back on itself where a cable would bow. There is no air, no stretch, no twist and no third dimension — every strand is in the same flat plane, which is why they pass through each other rather than tangling. Only the bulbs collide, and only with each other: a bulb and somebody else's flex do not notice one another at all. Your hand is not a hand: it draws everything near it inward, which nothing you could put in a room does, and it is the only thing in here that can push a flex at all.

    The flexes are not cut the way anybody would cut them. Each one is a link longer or shorter than the one beside it and never more, so the drops wander gently along the rail rather than varying as they please. That is not decoration: a bulb hanging a hundred units clear of its neighbours can never touch one, so it can never change colour or pass its own on, and a festoon with two of those in it cannot be finished. Lengths drawn freely do that about as often as not.

    The glass is not glass either. A bulb cracks on the speed two of them meet at and on nothing else — not the angle, not the weight, not where it was hit — and the damage stops at two cracks and darkness. Colour is settled on an instant in the same way: whichever bulb was going faster on the frame the two met has it, by however little, and a lean afterwards says nothing however hard either of them is pulled. The ring that marks a bulb as moving is drawn off a line across one number too, and means nothing to the rules — it is there to be looked at. Nothing falls, nothing swings free, nothing lands on the floor; a dark bulb hangs on its flex exactly as it did before. Light is drawn as a glow around the bulb and lights nothing else in the room.

    Colour is not mixed as light or as paint. The colours in play are a short list and a bulb is always exactly one of them, so Blend steps round that list rather than averaging anything; two bulbs meeting in the middle is arithmetic on a wheel of four or five positions, not on a spectrum.

    The simulation is a fixed step of a sixtieth of a second, and the only randomness is one seeded generator laying the festoon out and dealing the starting colours, so the same seed hangs the same festoon every time. What it cannot repeat is you: the hand is read live, so the same seed twice is the same lights and never the same run. Pausing stops everything, including your hand. Nothing is uploaded; your best time on each festoon and rule is kept in this browser.
