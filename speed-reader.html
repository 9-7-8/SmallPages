Speed Reader

Give it a link, or paste something in, and read it one word at a time, in place, at a pace you set. A link has the article pulled out of the page first — the menus, the footer and the comments dropped — the way reader mode does it. The focus letter is marked and every word is hung on it, so the word lands under your eye already lined up.

A browser may only fetch another site's page if that site says it can, and most don't, so the fallback is a public relay that hands the HTML back with the header the browser is after. The link goes to the relay; the article is still pulled out of the page here.

Space plays and pauses, the arrow keys step a word either way, ↑ and ↓ move the pace, and Home goes back to the start. The sentence under the word is there so that when you stop — and you will stop — you can see where you are without starting again. The pace is kept in this browser as soon as you move it, so the next page you read opens at the speed you left it.

How a link becomes an article. The page is fetched, parsed and scored in the tab: every block of text is weighed by how much prose it holds against how many links, menus and class names like sidebar or comment are thrown out, and the highest-scoring block, plus whatever sits alongside it, is the article. That is the algorithm behind Firefox's reader mode — Mozilla's Readability — written out in this page rather than loaded from anywhere, so the whole thing still runs in one file. The title, byline, site and date come from the page's own metadata where it has any. If the scoring picks the wrong block, the whole page switches to every word on it instead.

What the fetch costs. Pasting text keeps this page offline; a link cannot. The direct attempt goes straight from your browser to the site, which is the private one but works only on sites that allow it. A relay is a third-party service that fetches the page for you, so it sees the link — and it can be rate-limited or simply gone, which is why there are several and why you can put your own in. A site that renders its text with JavaScript, or sits behind a paywall or a login, hands back nothing worth reading whichever route is used: open it yourself and paste.

It does not hold the pace exactly. A long word is given a little longer than a short one, and a word that ends a clause, a sentence or a paragraph is held longer still, because reading punctuation at a flat rate is what makes this kind of thing feel like being shouted at. The pace you set is the average it works out to, not the gap between every pair.

What it costs. Reading this way removes the thing that makes reading reliable: going back. On straightforward prose at 400 or 500 words a minute most people lose little. On anything you would normally re-read a line of — a contract, a proof, poetry — comprehension falls off a cliff, and no pace setting fixes that. It is a tool for getting through the inbox, not for studying.
