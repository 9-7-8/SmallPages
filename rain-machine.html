Rain Machine

Rain built from scratch in the page, with a map of the weather making it. You are the dot in the middle of three kilometres; cloud drifts over you on the wind, and what you hear depends on where it is. Thick cloud overhead is drops on whatever is above your head; thick cloud a kilometre away is a wash with the top taken off it by the air in between. Nothing sits still — each control is a band between two dots and the weather wanders inside it, so an hour of this does not flatten into one sound.

The weather

Nothing playing.

A drop is an impact, not a plink. Rain on a surface is a droplet exciting that surface's own resonances, so each one here is a noise burst a few milliseconds long fired into a bank of damped filters that stays up for the whole session — the panel is a fixed object, and only the hit changes. The banks are the figures from the research as tuning seeds, not measured specifications of any particular car: a roof at 170, 290, 470, 760 and 1,180 Hz ringing for 15 to 90 ms, a windshield at 650, 1,150, 1,900 and 3,100 decaying in 4 to 25, side glass at 850, 1,550 and 2,600 in 3 to 20. That is the difference between a hollow knock, a dry tick and a light lateral tap. The ground outside and the stone underground are my own, by the same method.

Timing is random, but not uniformly random. Drops arrive as a Poisson stream — the gap to the next is -ln(1 - r) / rate — because that is what a stream of independent events looks like, where an evenly jittered gap sounds mechanical. Strength is 0.1 + 0.9 r³, so small drops are common and heavy ones rare, and the properties are not rolled independently: a bigger drop gets a longer contact and more low end along with its loudness, because those go together on a real surface. Louder rain therefore means more drops as well as bigger ones, which is the actual difference between a drizzle and a downpour. Drop size is its own control so that turning the rain up does not silently also make every drop fatter.

Overhead and far away are different sounds. The cloud on the map is not decoration: the audio reads it. Thickness directly above you sets how fast drops land on whatever is over your head, and everything further out becomes a wash with the top rolled off, because a kilometre of air absorbs the high frequencies first. So you can stand in a gap with it hammering down half a mile away, and hear exactly that — sparse ticks close by over a dense, dull roar in the distance.

Nothing holds still. Rain, wind and thunder are each a band between two dots rather than one value, and the weather wanders inside its band on a slow random walk; the bright mark on the bar is where it is now. Wind and rain are tied together, as they are outside — a gust pushes the rain up with it and the lull afterwards takes it back down, so the squalls arrive and pass rather than the level simply sitting. Restlessness sets how fast it all moves. This is the part aimed squarely at fatigue: a constant noise stops being heard and starts being endured, and a sound that keeps changing slightly does not.

Four places to stand, and the difference is what is in the way. Each surface is filtered as itself rather than one low-pass being dropped over the whole mix. In a car the roof is a steel sheet a foot above you and stays loud and central, the windshield sits in its own narrow place, side glass goes to the side it is on, and the exterior wash is cut hard. A house takes more off again and swaps the metal for tile and glass. Underground almost nothing survives above a few hundred hertz: a rumble through earth, slow drips on stone, and a long tail on them, generated here as noise under an exponential decay rather than recorded.

Thunder is an extended event, not one bang. A flash lights up a channel kilometres long, and you hear the near end of it before the far end, which is what turns a crack into a roll. Each strike here is several segments at different distances along the channel, each arriving at flash + distance / 343 and each filtered for how far it came — the nearest keeps its tearing onset, the rest fill in the body and the rolling tail behind it. The ring on the map leaves the strike at that same 343 metres a second, so it reaches the middle on the tick the sound does: a strike at the edge takes nearly nine seconds, and counting the ring in is counting the real thing.

Nothing is a recording. No downloads, no loops, and the noise the whole thing is built on is crossfaded end to beginning so there is no seam to hear.
