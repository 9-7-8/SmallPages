Image Upscaler

Drop in a picture and it comes back bigger, with the detail guessed by a super-resolution network rather than smeared by a stretch. The small fast model runs the moment the picture lands; the heavier one carries on behind it and takes the view when it finishes. Drag the seam to put the browser's own stretch next to what the model made. Both models and the runtime are in this repository, so after the first load it needs no connection, and the picture never leaves the tab.

Fast is Real-ESRGAN's realesr-general-x4v3, the small general-purpose network its authors ship for exactly this — about a megabyte and a half of weights, and the one that answers while you are still looking at the page. It is a 4× model, and it is what runs on every picture the moment it lands.

High quality is Real-CUGAN, a heavier network from bilibili with its own 2× and 4× weights, in the conservative variant — the one that does the least to the picture beyond enlarging it. It costs two to three times the fast model's time, so it starts only once the fast answer is on screen, and it takes the view when it arrives unless you have picked a side yourself. It holds a hard edge and a flat area better, which is what it was trained for; on a photograph the two are closer than the labels suggest, and the seam is there to settle it rather than take our word for it. Both are in this repo, converted to TensorFlow.js by @plotdb/upscaler from the official weights, and they run on your GPU through WebGL where the browser offers it and on the CPU where it does not.

The sizes. A model enlarges by the factor it was trained for and nothing else, so the other sizes are reached around it. 4× is one pass of the 4× weights and nothing more. 2× runs the 4× pass and then halves the result, which is better than a 2× model would be on its own, because every output pixel is an average of four the network drew — except on the quality model, which has real 2× weights and uses them. 8× is two passes with a resample in between. Target… takes any size at all: the two boxes stay in proportion, the smallest pass that covers the gap runs, and the last step down to the exact figure is an ordinary resample. Ask for something smaller than the original and you get that resample on its own, because no model can add detail to a picture you are shrinking.

Tiles. The converted models take a fixed 64 × 64 patch, so a picture is walked in overlapping tiles and the overlaps are feathered back together: a tile's edge is where a network knows least, and butting them up unfeathered leaves a grid you cannot unsee. That is also why the work is counted in tiles rather than in seconds, and why a big picture takes a while — the cost is per pixel of the output, so every step up doubles the width and quadruples the work. Sixteen megapixels is the ceiling here; past that the accumulators alone are a quarter of a gigabyte, so a size that would overshoot it is turned off rather than attempted.

Served, not opened. The models run in a worker so that a few hundred tiles of arithmetic don't take the page down with them, and a browser will not start a worker from a file:// folder. So this is one of the pages here that has to be served — python3 -m http.server in the repo and open it over http. It says so if you open it the other way. After that first load nothing else is fetched: the runtime and both sets of weights are in the repo, and your browser keeps the compiled models in its own storage besides.

What you are comparing. The left of the seam is the original drawn at the output's size by the browser itself, which is the same smooth stretch you would get from an image editor's default — not the original at its own size, which would be a comparison of two different pictures. The right is the model's answer. Set it to 1:1 and the stage scrolls rather than fits, which is where the difference stops being a feeling. Transparency is carried through, but the models only see colour: an alpha channel rides along as a plain resize, so a hard-edged cutout comes back with a soft edge.
