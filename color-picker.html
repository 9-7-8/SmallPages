Pull the colours out of an image, or grow them from the colour you have, and lay them out to one of the classic harmony rules. Click any swatch to make it the current colour.

Leave Colours empty and the picture decides how many: five unless it has fewer colours than that, or more that are each worth a place.

Colours are clustered in OKLab, so what comes back is what the eye would call dominant rather than what the RGB numbers average to. The clustering deliberately finds more colours than anybody wants — a picture of a forest has a dozen greens and they are all the same green — and then the nearest two are combined, over and over, the one covering more of the picture keeping its colour — so a speck is absorbed into the region it sits on without tinting it, and every colour you get back is one that is actually in the picture rather than an average of two that are not. What adds up is the coverage: the percentage under a swatch is every shade folded into it. Dominant stops combining when the colours left are ones a person would call different; every other rule gets the same combined set to choose from.

Leaving Colours empty lets the picture decide. It aims at five: fewer when the picture has fewer colours that stay apart, more while each extra one still holds a twentieth of the frame, and never more than twelve. Type a number and it combines as far as that number instead — which past a point means folding together colours that are not really alike, so the swatch says how many shades went into it and the one you see is the commonest of them. Under a rule rather than Dominant, an empty box takes the count from the rule's own geometry: two for complementary, three for triadic, four for square.

Under a rule, each slot is filled by the image colour closest to the hue that rule asks for; when the image has nothing near it, the nearest colour is rotated onto the target hue instead and marked turned, which is as close to the rule as that image can honestly get.
