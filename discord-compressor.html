Discord Compressor

A file too big for the upload box, brought under it. A video is re-encoded — a different codec, fewer pixels, fewer frames a second, quieter audio, whichever of those you want — at a bitrate worked backwards from the size you have to hit, in whichever of H.264, VP9, AV1, HEVC and VP8 your browser will write, so it works on a phone as well as at a desk. A picture is re-encoded the same way. Anything else is deflated, and split into parts if even that will not do it. All of it in the tab: nothing is uploaded to anybody, which rather defeats the object otherwise.

    Why the size lands where it does. A video's size is its bitrate times its length, so the sum runs the other way: the limit gives the bits, the length divides them, and what is left after the sound is the picture's budget. An encoder asked for a bitrate does not hit it exactly — measured here it came out anywhere from 74% to 121% of the figure it was given, depending on the codec and how hard the footage is — so three bitrates are encoded at once off a single read of the video and the largest result that fits is the one kept. The encoding is the cheap part; reading the frames out is not, which is why it is done once rather than three times. Footage close to white noise can beat all three — static, confetti, a screen full of snow — and then it reads the video a second time at bitrates aimed with what the first read measured: twice the wait, in the one case where the alternative is a file you cannot send.

    It will not spend a budget it does not need. A 4 MB clip given a 50 MB limit comes back at about 4 MB, not 50. Re-encoding upwards cannot put back what the first encoder threw away, so the bitrate the file arrived with is a ceiling on the bitrate it leaves with, and the line under the controls says when that ceiling is the one doing the work rather than the limit.

    It writes whatever this browser will write. The codec box is built by asking the browser's own encoder, one codec at a time, which of them it will take — and the container follows from that, because the two are not independent. H.264 and HEVC can only go in an MP4; VP8 can only go in a WebM; VP9 and AV1 go in either, and there the box beside the codec is yours to pick. VP9 in a WebM is the smallest file for the quality, which is why it is the default where it exists. H.264 in an MP4 is the one everything on earth will open, and on an iPhone or an iPad it is the only thing there is: every browser on iOS is Safari underneath, and Safari's encoder writes H.264 and HEVC and nothing else. A page that offered VP9 alone — which is what this one did until somebody tried it on a phone — simply said "no video encoder" to half the people who needed it.

    And where there is no encoder at all, it records instead. Firefox has no VideoEncoder — WebCodecs is newer than Firefox's MediaRecorder by about a decade — so there the clip is played and recorded rather than encoded frame by frame. It works, and it has one cost worth knowing before you start: a recording runs at the speed the thing plays, so a two-minute clip is a two-minute wait, and twice that if the first attempt comes out over the limit and has to be aimed again. The sound is taken off the video through a graph that is deliberately not plugged into your speakers, so nothing plays out loud at you while it works. Sound on its own goes the same way where there is nothing to encode it with.

    What your browser will actually do. Nothing above is a list of browsers, because a list of browsers goes out of date between one release and the next and cannot be checked from here anyway. Every codec is asked for by name, at the size and bitrate about to be used, and only the ones that answer yes are offered. If the box says there is nothing, that is the browser's own answer and not a guess about it — and node cli/run.mjs discord-compressor limits prints every string that was tried and what came back — both halves, the encoder's and the recorder's — which is the thing to read out when a file that compresses on one machine will not compress on another.

    Reading the video is the slow part. The page steps through the source a frame at a time, seeking to each one and drawing it, because that is the only way to read a video a browser will play without a demuxer. Roughly real time on a short clip, and slower than real time on a long one at a high frame rate — a half-hour video is not what this is for, and on a browser with no WebCodecs it is exactly as long as the clip. Dropping to 15 or 24 frames a second makes it quicker as well as smaller, since a frame not wanted is a frame not read.

    Twice is worse than once. Everything here but the deflate and the split is lossy, and lossy on top of lossy compounds: a clip that has already been through this once and comes back for another pass is being quantised on top of quantisation. Go back to the original each time. The same goes for a video somebody else already squeezed to fit — there is not much left in it to take.

    What it cannot read. Only what the browser can. A video it cannot play and a picture it cannot decode come out the other end as a file to deflate or split — and it says that is what happened, naming the decoder rather than filing your clip under "a bag of bytes", because a browser without H.264 is a browser that will not open most MP4s. An animated GIF or WebP needs ImageDecoder to be read frame by frame, and Safari has not got one, so on an iPhone an animation is recognised from its bytes and you are told that compressing it here would leave you with its first frame — which is worth a sentence rather than a surprise.

    Splitting is not compression. It is the last thing on the list for a reason: the parts are useless until somebody puts them back together, and that somebody has to be told how. The line under the parts is the command to paste — cat on a Mac or Linux, copy /b on Windows. Discord takes ten attachments a message, so ten parts is one message and eleven is two.
