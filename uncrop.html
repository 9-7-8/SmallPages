Uncrop

Put a picture on a bigger canvas and have the empty border painted in. Say what size you want, move the picture about inside it, and an inpainting model invents the rest — the sky above, the carpet below, the wall either side of a portrait that was cropped too tight. It runs on your own machine: the model's weights are fetched once and cached, and the picture never leaves the page. Your own pixels are kept exactly as they were, so what comes back is your photograph with new edges rather than a redrawing of it.

What it is doing

Your pixels are kept. The model only ever paints where there was nothing. When it has finished, your picture is drawn back over the result at its full resolution, so every pixel you brought is the pixel you brought — an inpainting network reconstructs what it was shown as well as what it was not, and its version of your photograph is slightly softer than your photograph. Only the border is its work. The one exception is the outermost two pixels of your picture, which are blended with what meets them so that the join is not a line; everything inside that comes back byte for byte, and the test suite checks it.

It works at 512 px and the border is enlarged. Both models see a 512-pixel-square view, which is what they were trained at, so the whole canvas is shrunk to fit that, filled, and the new border is then scaled up to meet your picture. That is the trade this page makes to run in a tab: the invented part is soft and gets softer the bigger the canvas, while your own picture stays sharp. It continues sky, water, grass, carpet, plaster and bokeh well; it will not invent a face, a hand, or a straight line that has to meet something far away, and a long thin extension — a 16:9 canvas round a portrait — is the hardest case there is.

A big jump is made in steps. Asked to double a picture, neither model has anything to go on out at the far corner, so the border is grown in passes, each one adding at most a third of what it can already see to any side and then becoming the known picture for the next. Half again is one pass, twice is two, four times is three and ten times is five; eight is the limit, and past that the last pass is handed whatever is left, which smears. The count is on screen before you start, because each pass is a model run — roughly a second each on a GPU and ten or twenty without one.

Best model is LaMa — big-lama, through Carve's ONNX export, about 206 MB, and it is what the page opens on. Its fast-Fourier convolutions see the whole frame at once rather than a neighbourhood, which is why it is the one that holds a horizon straight and keeps a texture's scale across a wide band; in use the fast model's borders came back visibly worse, which is why this one is the default rather than the smaller download. It is two and a half times the download and several times the work per pass. LaMa's code is Apache 2.0; the weights were trained on Places365-Challenge, whose own terms are research and education, so if this is for something commercial the fast model is the one with the clean licence.

Fast model is MI-GAN (ICCV 2023, Picsart AI Research), the pipeline ONNX export at about 80 MB, fetched from Hugging Face the first time you ask for it and then kept in your browser's storage. It was built to inpaint on a phone, so it is quicker on a slow machine and the smaller download, and both the code and the weights are MIT licensed.

Adding a strip. The four Add fields are the direct way to say "this much more on the left": a number is pixels, and a percentage is a share of the picture — of its width on the left and right and of its height on the top and bottom, unless %w or %h says otherwise, so 15%h on the left of a book cover is a strip as wide as fifteen per cent of the cover is tall. The canvas grows to fit and the picture sits where the border leaves it, so nothing has to be worked out by hand.

A batch. Choose or drop several pictures at once and the first one opens here to be set up; what you set is then used on every one of them in turn, with the progress on screen, and they are saved together as one .zip, each named after the picture it came from — one file rather than a download per picture, which browsers cancel when they come in a burst. The line under the drop zone says how each picture will be treated. A border typed into the Add fields as a percentage is the setting that suits a pile of pictures of different sizes; a canvas size, a shape or a placement on the pad is used as it stands, and the model is loaded once for the lot.

No model needs no download and happens instantly. It mirrors your picture out across each edge and melts the reflection into a blur as it gets further away, which is an old trick and an honest one: on grass, gravel, fabric, fog or a blurred background it is genuinely hard to catch, and on anything with structure in it — a horizon, a doorway, a face near the edge — it is obviously a mirror. It is also what you get when there is no WebGPU and no connection, and it is a reasonable way to see whether your framing is right before spending the download.

Where it runs. The models are ONNX, run by ONNX Runtime Web from jsDelivr, on WebGPU where the browser has it and on the CPU through WebAssembly where it does not. Some graphics cards start a model and then fail part way through a run — LaMa's Fourier layers have been seen to on an older NVIDIA card — so a run that fails on the GPU is started again on the CPU by itself, the status line says so, and that model stays on the CPU until the page is reloaded. The CPU path works and is maybe ten times slower, on one thread, because more than one needs cross-origin isolation headers that a static host does not send. Neither the runtime nor the weights are in this repository, so the first run of each model needs a connection; after that the weights sit in your browser's own storage and it does not. The page has to be served rather than opened off the filesystem, because WebAssembly will not load from a file:// folder.

How big is too big. Anything up to 16384 pixels on a side is allowed, because that is the largest canvas browsers will give; above about 12 megapixels the page says so, because every copy of the canvas is four bytes a pixel and it holds a few at once, and a phone will give up long before a desktop does. 64 megapixels is the ceiling here. The warning is about your machine rather than about the model: the fill is painted at 512 px whatever the canvas, so a bigger canvas costs memory and time without buying any more invented detail.

Saving. PNG is lossless and reads everywhere; it is the right default. TIFF is written here by hand, in strips with Deflate compression, because no browser can encode one and it is what the rest of the photographic world wants for a big lossless image — Photoshop, Affinity, GIMP, Preview and ImageMagick all open it, and it carries the resolution so a print at 300 dpi comes out the size it should. WebP is a good deal smaller for the same picture but cannot exceed 16383 pixels on a side; the slider at 100 asks for lossless and Chromium gives exactly that, which the suite pins, while anything below it is lossy; JPEG is the one to pick when the file has to be small and nothing after this will edit it. The quality slider only appears for the two that have one.
