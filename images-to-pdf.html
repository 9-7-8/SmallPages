Images → Searchable PDF

Drop a pile of photographs or scans and get back one document you can search. Each picture gets a page of its own, laid on white paper with an inch of margin and nothing cropped off, and the words that were read out of it are written underneath it in ink you cannot see — so the page looks exactly like the photograph and still answers Ctrl+F. The reading happens in this tab, and it is a machine reading handwriting and shadows, so there is a box for fixing what it got wrong.

The picture is placed, not cropped. Each page is a sheet of the paper you chose with the margin you set taken off every side, and the picture is scaled by whichever of width or height runs out first, then centred both ways. Nothing is cut off and nothing is stretched, so a tall receipt and a wide spread both come out whole on the same size of page.

The text goes in invisible. Every word is written over the place it was read from in PDF text rendering mode 3, which draws nothing at all, so the page you look at is the photograph and the page a search looks at is the words. Each word is squeezed or stretched to the width of the box it came from, so dragging to select lands roughly where you would expect rather than drifting along the line.

A JPEG goes in untouched where it can. PDF carries JPEG natively, so a baseline JPEG is embedded exactly as it arrived — no decoding, no re-encoding, no second generation of loss. A progressive JPEG cannot go in that way, because PDF's decoder does not read them, so those are re-encoded along with PNGs and everything else. Choose lossless and the pixels are deflated instead, which keeps small text crisp and makes a much bigger file.

Corrections move the words, within reason. The reading comes back as lines with a box each. When you edit the text, your lines are matched to those boxes in order, so fixing a word keeps it where it was; adding lines past the end of what was read stacks them under the last box. Rewriting a page completely will leave the invisible text in roughly the right place rather than exactly, which costs nothing for searching and shows only if you drag to select.

Markdown is a best effort and says so. Paragraphs are kept, and a line set noticeably larger than the rest of its page becomes a heading. It does not try to rebuild tables or columns out of a photograph, because that is guessing dressed as structure. The plain text output does none of this: it is the words, in order, and nothing else.

A long document is the ordinary case. A hundred and more scans make a hundred-and-more-page document: the pictures are read one at a time, the bar says which one, and nothing here caps how many go in. A document that long is worth setting the quality on first — a hundred photographs at full camera resolution is a file nobody can open, which is what smaller is for. If what you have is already a PDF of scans rather than a pile of pictures, PDF OCR is the page for it: it reads the document you have and gives the same one back with the words in it.

It runs here, and it is a big download the first time. The reader is Tesseract.js with the English model, about 7 MB of engine and training data in vendor/tesseract/. It is in the repo rather than fetched from anyone's CDN, so no picture and no text ever leaves the tab — but it does mean this page is the one here that wants to be served rather than opened straight off the filesystem, because a browser will not start a worker from a file:// folder. The page says so if you open it that way.
