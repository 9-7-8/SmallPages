Dice Roller

Type the roll the way you would say it — 2d6, 5d6 + 2d3, d20 + 7 — and watch it land. Every die is a real solid, thrown across the table and left to stop where it stops. Underneath, the same roll thrown ten thousand times over, so you can see where yours sits in it.

The shapes are the real ones. A die has to be able to land on one face and show you another, which is a stronger thing to ask than having the right number of faces. Where a solid does it, that is what you get: a cube, an octahedron, a dodecahedron, an icosahedron, and the kite-faced trapezohedron that a d10 and a d14 actually are. Where half the number of faces is even, a bipyramid instead — two cones of triangles meeting at the equator — which is how a d8 and a d16 are made. The four is the exception that real ones make too: it rests on a face and is read at the corner pointing up, so the number sits at the corners rather than in the middle of a face.

An odd number of sides is twice as many faces. No solid with an odd number of faces can be read at all — land a three-sided prism on a face and what is on top of it is an edge. So a d3 is a cube carrying 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, which is exactly what a d3 you can buy is, and a d5 and a d7 are ten- and fourteen-sided solids with each number on two opposite faces. Past twenty-four faces, and for odd numbers past eleven, there is nothing honest left to build: those are rolled properly and landed on an icosahedron with the number they rolled written on the face that came up, the other nineteen carrying numbers from the same range for company.

The throw is not steered. The dice are dropped in, bounced off the walls and each other and left to stop where they stop — every contact is an impulse at the corner that is touching, which is why landing on a corner flips a die over it and a skid turns into a roll. Nothing tips them onto a chosen face at the end. What is decided in advance is the number: it is drawn before the throw starts, and the painting is what is fitted to it, once the dice have stopped and it is known which face each one came up on. Which number sits on which face is arbitrary on a real die too — it is settled in a factory, not by the throw — and the arrangement is kept: opposite faces still add to one more than the number of sides, or carry the same number where each number is on two faces.

The figures are under both. Under the dice: how many were thrown, the pips between them, and the mean, the median and the ends of what they showed — the dice only, because a flat +3 is not a die and has no average to take. Under the chart: the same for the twenty thousand throws behind it — how many, the mean, the median, the total that came up most often, the spread either side and the lowest and highest ever reached, with the chance of doing at least as well as the throw on the table.

Two buttons by the table and one above it. Roll beside the dice is the Roll at the top of the page again, for when the table is where you are looking; Throw again throws the dice you have already rolled rather than rolling new ones, which is the one way to see the same numbers land differently. Turn Throw them off and the dice are set down showing their numbers with no throw at all — which is also what happens, without asking, if your system is set to ask for reduced motion.

The numbers are painted on, not stuck to your eye. Each face carries its number at a fixed angle in the die's own frame, standing on one of that face's edges, so it turns as the die turns and comes up sideways or upside down exactly as the numbers on a real die do. The throw is run through once before anything is drawn, on a fixed clock, so the page knows where every die stops; then the same throw is run again in front of you and comes out the same way, because nothing in it is random after it starts.

The numbers come from the system's own randomness. crypto.getRandomValues rather than Math.random, and the remainder is taken only from the part of the range that divides evenly — draws that fall in the ragged tail are thrown away and drawn again. Without that last step a d6 taken as random % 6 gives the low faces a hair more often than the high ones, which is small enough never to be noticed and still a loaded die.

Every new roll is thrown ten thousand times over. Roll something this page has not simulated yet and it runs ten thousand of them while you look at the dice, so the chart underneath is always of the roll on the table rather than of whatever was charted last. Roll the same thing again and it leaves the chart alone, including a bigger run you asked for yourself. A million throws is what you get for pressing Simulate. Leave the box empty for that, or type a number for any other count, up to ten million. The chart is every total that came up and how often, as a share of the throws rather than a count, so the shape is the same whether you ran a thousand or a million; the mark across it is the roll sitting on the table above. Where there are more totals than there are columns of pixels — a hundred dice have ninety thousand of them — neighbouring totals share a bar, and the caption under the chart says how many to a bar. Point at a bar, or walk it with the arrow keys, for that one's exact figures, and Show the numbers is the same thing as a table you can read or copy.

The simulation is not the roller. Rolling a hundred dice a million times is a hundred million numbers, and asking the operating system for them one at a time takes minutes. So the simulation seeds a fast generator — sfc32 — from crypto.getRandomValues and runs from there, still throwing away the ragged tail so each die stays even. It is worked in slices of twenty milliseconds, which is why the page still answers while the count climbs and why Stop stops it where it stands and charts what it has. The dice on the table above are never simulated; those are drawn one at a time from the system.

It will take anything you can say out loud. XdY terms and plain numbers, added and subtracted: 4d6 + 2, d20 - 1, 2d8 + 1d6 + 3. The X can be left off for one die. Up to a hundred dice in a roll and a thousand sides on any of them, after which the tray and the arithmetic both stop being useful. A die showing its highest face is lit in the breakdown and a 1 is dimmed, so a natural twenty reads from across the room.

It remembers. The roll you typed, whether the dice are thrown, and the last forty results are kept in this browser, on this page. Enter in the box rolls again. Nothing is sent anywhere.
